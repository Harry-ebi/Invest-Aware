Personal pensions

A personal pension is one you set up yourself with a pension provider, rather than through an employer. It works on the same principle as any pension: contributions go in, get invested, and are earmarked for later life, with the usual tax advantages and access rules.

People use personal pensions for all sorts of reasons — the self-employed, those without a workplace scheme, or anyone wanting an additional pot alongside a workplace pension.

What a SIPP is

SIPP stands for Self-Invested Personal Pension — a personal pension that typically offers a wider range of investment choices.

The “self-invested” part is the clue: a SIPP usually lets you choose from a broader menu of investments than a standard personal pension or a typical workplace scheme might. That flexibility can appeal to people who want more say over how their pension is invested — though more choice also means more responsibility for those decisions.

How it differs from a workplace scheme

  • Who arranges it. You set up a personal pension or SIPP yourself; a workplace pension is arranged through your employer.
  • Employer contributions. Workplace schemes generally include employer contributions; a personal pension typically does not (unless an employer agrees to pay into one).
  • Investment choice. A SIPP often offers a wider investment range, while workplace schemes commonly steer members into a default fund with some alternatives.
  • The wrapper principle still applies. In every case the pension is the account and the investments sit inside it.
No provider comparisons

InvestAware doesn't compare or recommend pension providers or SIPPs. Which type of pension suits someone — and how to invest it — depends on their circumstances, and may be a matter for regulated advice.
