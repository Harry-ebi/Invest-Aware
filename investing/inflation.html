Inflation

The quiet force that changes what your money is worth without changing how much of it you have.

The idea: purchasing power

£10,000 in twenty years is still £10,000 — but it probably won't buy as much.

Inflation is the general tendency for prices to rise over time. The number of pounds in your account can stay the same while the amount of goods and services those pounds can buy slowly shrinks. That shrinking is what we mean by a loss of purchasing power, and it's the main reason holding large amounts of cash for long periods carries a hidden cost.

Nominal return vs real return

Two words make this much clearer:

Nominal return

How much the number of pounds has increased. If £100 becomes £105, the nominal return is 5%.

Real return

How much your purchasing power has increased, after accounting for inflation. If prices also rose, the real return is smaller than the nominal one — and can even be negative.

This is why a savings rate that sounds fine in nominal terms can still leave you worse off in real terms if inflation is higher. It's the real return that determines what your money can actually do.

Inflation compounds too

Just like investment returns and costs, inflation compounds. A modest-sounding annual rate applies year after year, each time to a slightly higher base of prices — so the effect on purchasing power builds up significantly over decades.

The symmetry across the site

Returns compound (potentially growing wealth), costs compound (potentially reducing it), and inflation compounds (potentially reducing purchasing power). Small annual percentages become large over decades — which is why investing is generally framed as a long-term activity, and why leaving everything in cash has its own kind of risk.
