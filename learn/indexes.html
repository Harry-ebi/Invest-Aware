Indexes

The yardsticks you hear quoted on the news — and the blueprint behind index funds.

What it isExamplesIndex funds

An index is a defined way of measuring a particular market or group of investments.

An index follows a set of rules that decide which investments it includes and how much weight each one carries. That gives a single, consistent measure of how that slice of the market is doing overall. When you hear that “the market” rose or fell, it's usually an index being quoted.

Some well-known examples

FTSE 100

An index of 100 of the largest companies listed in the UK.

S&P 500

An index of 500 large companies listed in the United States.

MSCI World

An index spanning large and mid-sized companies across many developed countries.

These are named only as well-known examples of indexes — not as suggestions, recommendations or things to invest in.

From index to index fund

An index itself is just a measure — you can't invest in a number. But you can invest in a fund built to follow one:

An index fund tracks itholding the same investments, in similar proportions
You gain broad exposurea small piece of the whole market at once
The link

An index fund attempts to replicate the performance of an index — for example by holding the same investments in roughly the same proportions. It's a common, low-effort way to gain broad, diversified exposure to a whole market at once.

This is where indexes tie back into the site's bigger ideas: an index fund is a practical way to “buy the haystack”, and it sits at the heart of passive investing. Worth remembering, though: an index is only as broad as its rules — a narrow index gives narrow exposure, and even large indexes can be concentrated in a few big companies.
