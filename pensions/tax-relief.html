Pension tax relief

One of the main reasons pensions are treated as tax-advantaged — explained in principle, not in every technical detail.

The ideaThe limitsTax later

The principle

Broadly, tax relief means some of the money that would have gone to tax can instead go into your pension.

When you pay into a pension, you generally receive tax relief connected to the income tax you'd otherwise have paid on that money. The practical effect is that it can cost you less than the amount that ends up in your pension — because part of the contribution is, in effect, tax that's been redirected into your pot rather than paid to HMRC. Exactly how this is given depends on the scheme and your circumstances.

The idea in round numbers

A simple basic-rate example, to show the principle. Higher- and additional-rate taxpayers may be able to claim more, and how relief is given varies by scheme.

Amount that ends up in your pension£100
Provided by basic-rate tax relief (illustrative 20%)£20
So the cost to you is roughly£80

Illustrative only. Actual relief depends on your tax position, the type of scheme and current rules, all of which can change. This isn't a calculation of your own relief.

Related pieces

  • Employer contributions. In a workplace pension, money your employer pays in is on top of your own contributions — a separate benefit from tax relief.
  • Salary sacrifice. Some employers offer arrangements where you agree to a lower salary in exchange for higher pension contributions, which can change how tax and National Insurance apply. Whether it's available and how it works depends on the employer.

Limits apply

Tax relief isn't unlimited. There are allowances that cap how much you can contribute with tax advantages each year — the standard annual allowance is currently , though it can be reduced for some people (for example, high earners or those who have already flexibly accessed a pension). Because the details get intricate quickly, the key point for now is simply that limits exist.

Tax when you take it out

Pensions are often described as offering tax advantages “going in”. It's important to know that tax can apply “coming out” too: while a portion may typically be taken tax-free, the rest is generally taxable as income when you draw it. We cover this in accessing a pension.

Rules change — and this isn't advice

Pension tax rules are detailed and change from time to time, and their effect depends heavily on your personal circumstances. This page explains the principles only. Check current GOV.UK guidance, or take regulated advice, before acting.
