Investor behaviour

Some of the biggest risks to a long-term investor aren't in the markets at all. They're in how we react to them.

Our instincts weren't built for markets

The instinct to run from danger and towards safety serves us well in most of life. In investing, it can quietly work against us — because market prices don't behave like physical threats. What feels safe and what is sensible can pull in opposite directions.

The behavioural paradox

Consider how investing tends to feel at different moments:

After markets have risen

Investing often feels safe. Recent gains are reassuring, and it's tempting to put more in — sometimes right after prices have already climbed.

After markets have fallen

Investing often feels dangerous. Recent losses are frightening, and the urge is to pull money out — sometimes right after prices have already dropped.

But recent performance doesn't tell us what will happen next. Feeling safest after prices rise and most afraid after they fall sets up a familiar trap: buying after markets go up, and selling after they go down.

That's the opposite of what an investor would choose to do deliberately — and it's driven not by bad information but by ordinary emotion. Recognising the pattern is most of the defence against it. This links directly to why market timing is so difficult.

Common behavioural traps

None of these make someone foolish. They're well-documented tendencies that affect almost everyone at some point:

  • Selling after markets fall, crystallising a loss and potentially missing any subsequent recovery.
  • Buying after strong recent performance, assuming the recent past will simply continue.
  • Constantly changing strategy, so no approach is ever given time to work.
  • Following investment fashions and whatever is currently being talked about.
  • Trying to predict short-term market movements — see time in the market.
  • Making decisions based on headlines, which are written to be dramatic, not to guide long-term plans.
  • Taking on risk without understanding it, then being surprised by normal volatility.
  • Checking long-term investments far too often, which tends to amplify anxiety without adding useful information.
The message that ties it together

A sensible investment strategy is only useful if you can actually stick with it. A plan you'll abandon at the first frightening headline may be worse in practice than a simpler one you can hold through the ups and downs.

What helps

InvestAware won't tell you when to invest or what to buy. But understanding your own likely reactions in advance — and knowing that falls are a normal feature of investing rather than a sign it has stopped working — can make it easier to behave consistently. Deciding how you'll respond before markets fall is far easier than deciding in the middle of one.
