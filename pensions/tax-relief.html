Pension tax relief

One of the main reasons pensions are treated as tax-advantaged — explained in principle, not in every technical detail.

The principle

Broadly, tax relief means some of the money that would have gone to tax can instead go into your pension.

When you pay into a pension, you generally receive tax relief connected to the income tax you'd otherwise have paid on that money. The practical effect is that it can cost you less than the amount that ends up in your pension — because part of the contribution is, in effect, tax that's been redirected into your pot rather than paid to HMRC. Exactly how this is given depends on the scheme and your circumstances.

A relief-at-source, basic-rate example

In a relief-at-source pension, a basic-rate taxpayer might pay in £80 and have £100 credited to the pension after basic-rate tax relief is added.

You pay in£80
Basic-rate tax relief added (illustrative 20%)£20
Credited to your pension£100

Illustrative only, and specific to relief-at-source at the basic rate. Other pension arrangements apply relief differently (see below), higher- and additional-rate taxpayers may be able to claim more, and rules can change. This isn't a calculation of your own relief.

Related pieces

  • Employer contributions. In a workplace pension, your employer normally contributes as well under the terms of the scheme — a separate benefit from tax relief.
  • Different ways relief is given. The example above is “relief at source”. Under a net-pay arrangement, contributions come out before tax is calculated, so relief is effectively given a different way. The end result can be similar, but the mechanics differ.
  • Salary sacrifice. This is a separate arrangement, not another form of the same mechanism: you agree to a lower salary in exchange for higher employer pension contributions, which changes how tax and National Insurance apply. Whether it's available depends on the employer.

Limits apply

Tax relief isn't unlimited. There are allowances that cap how much you can contribute with tax advantages each year — the standard annual allowance is currently £60,000, though it can be reduced for some people (for example, high earners or those who have already flexibly accessed a pension). Because the details get intricate quickly, the key point for now is simply that limits exist.

Tax when you take it out

Pensions are often described as offering tax advantages “going in”. It's important to know that tax can apply “coming out” too: while a portion may typically be taken tax-free, the rest is generally taxable as income when you draw it. We cover this in accessing a pension.

Rules change — and this isn't advice

Pension tax rules are detailed and change from time to time, and their effect depends heavily on your personal circumstances. This page explains the principles only. Check current GOV.UK guidance, or take regulated advice, before acting.
