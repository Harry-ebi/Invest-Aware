Diversification

Investing's one “free lunch”: you don't need to know which investment wins next — you just need to own enough of them that it doesn't decide your future.

The one free lunch in investing

Don't bet everything on one outcome

Different investments do well at different times, and nobody reliably knows which will lead next. Diversification means you don't have to know.

Why it works

They don't all move together

When one part of a diversified portfolio is struggling, another may be doing something different. Combined, the bumps partly cancel out.

The combined line still moves — diversification doesn't make the ride smooth. But no single one of the four decides the outcome.

Diversification doesn't eliminate risk. It reduces the risk of being disastrously wrong about one thing.

Why call it a “free lunch”?

Less risk, without giving up expected return

Usually, aiming for more return means accepting more risk. Diversification is unusual: spreading out can cut the risk that comes from depending on one company, sector or country — without necessarily lowering your expected return.

Concentrated — a few holdings
Expected returnabout the same
Riskmore
Diversified — spread widely
Expected returnabout the same
Riskless

Conceptual — there's no single universal number for how much risk falls. And diversification can't remove market risk: if global markets fall, a diversified portfolio can still fall. What it reduces is the risk created by leaning too heavily on a particular company, sector, country or outcome.

Don't look for the needle. Buy the haystack.

A single broad market fund can give you exposure to hundreds or thousands of companies in one investment — the bottom of the money map, opened right up. This is a lesson about spreading risk, not a verdict that everyone should buy a tracker — active investing gets a fair hearing too.

The evidence

Beating the market is harder than it looks

Some managers really do beat the market. The difficult part is identifying them in advance — and them staying ahead over decades.

Over the ten years to mid-2025, about 87% of UK equity funds underperformed the market — only around 13 in 100 beat it.

An independent dataset points the same way: Morningstar's year-end 2025 barometer found only about 1 in 5 active funds survived and beat their average passive peer over the decade. The point isn't that active management never works — it can. It's that consistently beating a broad, low-cost market after fees has been very hard.

Sources: S&P Dow Jones Indices — SPIVA Europe (10 years to 30 June 2025: 87% of UK equity funds underperformed the S&P United Kingdom BMI); Morningstar Active/Passive Barometer, year-end 2025. Past performance does not predict future returns.

The other edge

Returns are uncertain. Costs aren't.

You can't control what markets return, but you can control what you pay. Every pound taken in fees is a pound that can no longer compound for you.
