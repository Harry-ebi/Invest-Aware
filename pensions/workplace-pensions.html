Workplace pensions

For many people this is where their investing journey quietly begins — often without them thinking of it as investing at all.

Who pays inHow it's investedWhy time matters so much

Who pays into a workplace pension

A workplace pension is typically built from more than one source of money:

  • Your contributions — usually a percentage of your pay, often taken automatically.
  • Your employer's contributions — money your employer adds on top, which is a genuine addition, not a deduction from your pay.
  • Tax relief — at a high level, some of the money that would have gone to tax can instead go into your pension. See pension tax relief.

Automatic enrolment

Under automatic enrolment, many employees are placed into a workplace pension by default, with the option to opt out. It's why a large number of people have a pension without ever having actively set one up. If you're enrolled, contributions from you and your employer are generally being paid in and invested on your behalf.

Where the money goes — the default fund

The money paid in doesn't sit as cash; it's invested. Most schemes place members in a default investment fund unless they choose otherwise, and many also offer a range of alternative investment choices. Remember the recurring idea: the pension is the account, and the fund is the investment inside it.

The clearest demonstration of compounding on the site

A workplace pension has the two ingredients compounding needs most: regular contributions and, often, decades of time. Money paid in when someone is young may stay invested for thirty or forty years. Here's the chain:

The key educational point

A pension pot isn't simply the sum of the money paid into it. Over a long enough period, investment growth can account for a substantial proportion of the eventual value — potentially more than the contributions themselves. See how compounding produces that shape on compound growth.

This describes how compounding can work; it is not a promise about any particular pension. Investment values fall as well as rise, returns are not guaranteed, and outcomes depend on contributions, charges, how the money is invested and the length of time invested.

A few things worth knowing

Contribution levels, how tax relief is given, and the investment options available all vary between schemes. Costs still matter here as they do anywhere — charges within a pension compound too. And because access to a pension is generally restricted until a minimum age, it's money earmarked for the long term, which is part of what makes the long compounding horizon possible.

Not advice

Whether to opt out, how much to contribute, and how to invest a pension are personal decisions that depend on your circumstances. InvestAware explains how these things work but doesn't tell you what to choose. Tax rules and limits can change.
