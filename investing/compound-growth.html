Compound growth

Probably the single most important idea on InvestAware — and one of the few in finance that genuinely earns the word “powerful”.

What compounding is

Compounding is a simple chain of events that repeats:

Your money can generate returns. Those returns then stay invested — and can generate returns of their own.

That's the whole idea. What makes it striking isn't any single year; it's what happens when the chain repeats for a long time. Later returns are being earned not just on the money you originally put in, but on all the returns that came before.

Watch the shape of the curve

The chart below illustrates someone paying in a steady amount each month. The lower line is simply the total they've paid in. The upper line is an illustrative value if those contributions grew at a steady rate. Early on, the two lines are close together — the growth looks modest. Later, the gap widens dramatically. That gap is compounding: returns earning returns.

Paying in £200 a month, illustrated over 35 years

Assuming a steady 5% a year before costs and inflation — an illustration, not a forecast.

The important feature is the shape: the upper line curves upward and pulls away from the money paid in, because previous returns are themselves generating returns.

The 5% figure is a round number chosen to illustrate the mechanism. Real investment returns vary from year to year, are not guaranteed, and can be negative. This is not a prediction of what any particular investment will do.

Why does it matter? Because time does the heavy lifting

If early growth looks unexciting, that's normal — and it's exactly why compounding rewards patience. Most of the eventual growth in a long-term investment tends to arrive later, once there's a large base of previous returns to build on. Interrupting that process early can matter more than it first appears.

The recurring InvestAware idea

Small decisions repeated over long periods can have surprisingly large consequences. That's as true of costs and inflation as it is of returns — those compound too.

Starting earlier gives compounding longer to work

The clearest way to see the effect of time is to hold everything else constant and change only when someone starts. Consider two people paying in the same amount, at the same illustrative return, both stopping at 60.

Investor A

Starts investing £200 a month at 25.

Investor B

Starts investing £200 a month at 35.

Investor B pays in for 25 years; Investor A for 35 years — ten years more. But the difference in the illustrative outcome at 60 is larger than that ten-year head start might suggest, because Investor A's earliest contributions have the longest time to compound.

The lesson

The point isn't “you must invest right now.” Everyone's circumstances, timeframe and need for cash are different. The point is simply that time gives compounding more opportunity to work — which is why starting earlier, when you're able to, can matter so much.

Figures are illustrative and assume contributions and returns that would not occur so smoothly in reality. They are not forecasts or recommendations.

Compounding happens through the investment

It's worth being precise about where compounding occurs. Returns are generated down at the investment and its underlying assets — company profits, dividends, interest. The wrapper (an ISA or pension) affects the tax around it; the compounding itself happens below.

Returns from the investment can stay invested and generate further returns — which is why nothing here depends on what the account is called.
