Bonds

If a share is owning part of a company, a bond is closer to lending it money.

What it isTypesThe risks

Buying a bond generally means lending money to a government or company.

In return for that loan, the borrower typically promises two things: to pay interest along the way, and to repay the amount borrowed at a set future date. Bonds are often described as “fixed income” because of those regular interest payments.

You lendbuy the bond — lend to a government or company
You receive interestregular payments over the bond's life
You're repaidthe amount borrowed is returned at the end

That's the intended path. The risks below are the ways it can differ in practice.

Two common kinds

Government bonds

Loans to a government. In the UK these are known as gilts. Bonds issued by financially strong governments are often regarded as relatively lower-risk — though “lower” is not “no” risk.

Corporate bonds

Loans to companies. These usually offer higher interest than strong government bonds, reflecting the greater chance that the borrower runs into trouble.

The main risks

  • Credit risk. The borrower might not be able to pay the interest or repay the loan. Different borrowers carry different levels of this risk.
  • Interest-rate risk. The market value of a bond can move as interest rates change — broadly, when rates rise, the value of existing bonds tends to fall, and vice versa.

Because bonds tend to behave differently from shares, they're often used alongside them in a portfolio — a point picked up in asset allocation.

Keeping it simple

Bonds can get intricate, but for a first understanding this is enough: you lend, you receive interest, you're repaid at the end — with real risks that the value moves or the borrower can't pay.
