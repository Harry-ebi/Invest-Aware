Investment costs

Costs are one of the few things about the future an investor can see clearly in advance. That's exactly why they're worth understanding.

What are the costs?In pounds, not just percentWhy they compound

Where costs come from

Investing usually involves several kinds of charge. Exactly which apply depends on how you invest, but the common ones are:

  • Platform or account fees — what a provider charges to hold your account, often a percentage of the amount held or a flat fee.
  • Fund charges — the ongoing cost of running a fund, usually quoted as a percentage per year.
  • Trading costs — charges when investments are bought or sold.
  • Advice fees — where you pay for personal financial advice (which InvestAware does not provide).

Translate the percentage into pounds

Percentages can feel abstract. Turning them into pounds makes them concrete:

A 0.5% annual fee is £5 for every £1,000 invested, each year — before allowing for any change in the value of the investment.

Seen this way, the gap between a low-cost and a higher-cost option is just a few pounds per thousand each year. Over a single year, that can look trivial. Over decades, it's a different story — because of compounding.

Returns compound. Costs can compound too.

Here's the part that's easy to miss. A fee isn't simply this year's small deduction. The money taken in charges is money that would otherwise have stayed invested — and could itself have gone on to generate returns. So a cost doesn't just remove a slice today; it also removes all the future growth that slice might have produced.

The symmetry worth remembering

Investment returns compound, potentially helping wealth grow. Costs compound too, potentially reducing it. And, as it happens, inflation compounds as well. Small annual percentages become significant over long periods — in every direction.

These figures are illustrative. They assume a single lump sum and a perfectly steady return, which real investing does not provide. They're meant to show how cost differences behave over time, not to predict any outcome. Lower cost does not guarantee a better result, and cost is only one of several things worth weighing.

Keeping it fair

Cost matters — but “cheapest” isn't automatically “best”. Some investments aim to do things a low-cost tracker can't, and may charge more for it. The point of this page is simply that costs deserve attention, because they're predictable and they compound. We don't rank providers.
